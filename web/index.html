How to use this page

This page answers one question: if a lander sat at this spot, would it get sunlight and a radio link to Earth — and when? Everything is computed from two NASA datasets: the LOLA terrain map (heights) and the DE421 ephemeris (Sun and Earth positions).

1 · Pick a place

Choose a Site and Region in the top bar, click anywhere on the map, or type a latitude and longitude in the Site card and press Go. The white crosshair is the observer. Drag to pan, scroll to zoom. The outlined shapes are candidate landing regions from NASA's Product 78 catalogue.

Coordinates are signed: south is negative latitude, west is negative longitude. Example: -89.44, -137.09.

2 · Read the Site card

  • Region — the landing region containing the point, or “custom point”.
  • Parent site — the named group, e.g. Site01 “Connecting ridge”.
  • Latitude / Longitude — position on the Moon. Negative latitude is south; 90°S is the pole.
  • Ground height — terrain elevation above the 1,737.4 km reference sphere, in metres.
  • Region area — size of the landing region polygon.
  • Map position — x / y on the south-polar map, in metres from the pole.
  • Distance to pole — straight-line distance to 90°S.
  • Go to coordinates — jump to any typed latitude/longitude inside the terrain file.

3 · Save and compare scenarios

Set up a site and date, then press Save current in the Scenarios card. Change the site, date, window or mast, and save a second scenario. Press Compare to open a side-by-side table of sunlit time, Earth visibility, longest sunlit run, elevation ranges and the next Sun/Earth event. Green marks the best value in each row.

Up to six scenarios are kept in your browser, so they survive a reload. Use Load to return to one, or × to remove it.

4 · Read the Terrain horizon

This is a view looking straight up. The centre is the observer, the rim is the horizon, and N/E/S/W show the compass. The shaded shape is how high the ground rises in each direction, in degrees above level. The 0° ring is flat ground; anything inside the shape is hidden behind terrain.

  • Highest obstacle — the tallest ridge and the direction it lies in.
  • Mean horizon — average ridge height all the way around.
  • Above Sun's max — the share of directions where terrain is taller than the Sun ever gets. Above 0% means some ground never sees direct sunlight.
  • Observer height — ground height plus the mast you set.

Why it matters: near the south pole the Sun never climbs much above 2°, so a 3° ridge can keep a site dark for a whole lunar day.

5 · Read the Sun & Earth chart

The two boxes above the chart are the now / next summary: whether the Sun and Earth are visible at the start of the window, their elevation, and how long until the next sunset, sunrise, earthset or earthrise. If no crossing happens inside the window, it says so.

Time runs left to right. Solid amber is the Sun's elevation; solid cyan is the Earth's. The dashed line of the same colour is the terrain horizon in the direction that body is currently in. When a solid line is above its dashed line, the body is visible. When it drops below, terrain blocks it. Pale amber bands mean sunlight; pale cyan bands mean an Earth link. Hover the chart to read exact values at any moment.

  • Sunlit / Earth visible — percentage of the window each one is visible.
  • Sunlit + linked — percentage with both at the same time.
  • Longest sunlit run — longest continuous stretch of light inside the window.
  • Sun / Earth elevation — the lowest and highest angle reached.
  • Horizon crossings — exact times the Sun or Earth rises above or sets behind terrain.

6 · Change the question

  • Start (UTC) — when the simulation begins.
  • Window — how long to simulate: 1 Earth day, 7 days, or one full lunar day (≈ 29.6 Earth days).
  • Step — time between samples. Smaller is smoother, but slower.
  • Mast — eye or antenna height above the ground. Raise it to see over nearby ridges.
  • Horizon range — how far away terrain is considered. Distant massifs matter at 25–50 km.

Good first experiments

  • Set Window to “1 lunar day” and press Recompute: watch the Earth swing ±6° across the sky.
  • Set Start to 2027-03-01: near the equinox the Sun actually rises and sets.
  • Raise Mast to 10 m: see how much horizon a taller antenna clears.
  • Click different regions: each one has a different horizon and a different answer.
  • Save the same site at two dates, press Compare, and see which date gives the better power/communication trade-off.

Green numbers are favourable; amber numbers are worth a closer look. A “—” means the value is still being calculated.
